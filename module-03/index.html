Module 03 — service and deployment models

Who patches the operating system? That is the whole question.

IaaS, PaaS and SaaS are not difficulty levels — they are different shares of the same seven-layer stack. Choose the wrong model, or misread your share of the work, and the breach is a configuration nobody thought they owned. This module draws the line, then draws it again for deployment.

01

Seven layers, and the line moves down as you climb the models

This is the diagram the module says you should be able to redraw from memory. Pick a model to isolate its column.

Read down a column and the pattern is arithmetic: 0, 3, 5, 6 of the seven layers go to the provider as you move from on-premises to SaaS. Read across the top row and it never moves. Data and access stay yours in all four — including SaaS, where the settings that decide who can share what are still your configuration, on your account.

security impact

Most cloud breaches are not the provider's stack failing — they are a layer the customer believed was covered. The matrix is a contract: whatever the cell says you, nobody else is checking.

02

Three models, component by component

What each one is actually built from, and what you get to stop worrying about.

IaaS

Infrastructure as a Service

components
Virtual machines, block and object storage, virtual networks, load balancers, hypervisor, control-plane APIs
examples
Amazon EC2 · Azure Virtual Machines · Google Compute Engine · Eucalyptus
you get
Maximum control. You patch the operating system.

PaaS

Platform as a Service

components
Language runtimes, middleware, managed databases, build and deploy tools, auto-scaling
examples
AWS Elastic Beanstalk · Google App Engine · Azure App Service · Heroku
you get
Focus on code. The provider patches the platform.

SaaS

Software as a Service

components
Multi-tenant application, web front end, tenant isolation, subscription and identity integration
examples
Microsoft 365 · Google Workspace · Salesforce · Zoom
you get
A ready-to-use application. You manage users, data and settings.

The test I use on anything new: if it breaks at 2am, whose pager goes off? Gmail is SaaS — mine never rings. A VM I built is IaaS — mine rings, and I am patching it.

03

The newer as-a-service models all move the same line

Each one hands another layer to the provider — and leaves your code, permissions and configuration exactly where they were.

Emerging models
ModelWhat it isExamplesWhat is still on you
FaaS / serverless Upload a function; the provider runs it on demand and bills per invocation. AWS Lambda, Azure Functions Function permissions and the code itself
CaaS / containers Run containers on managed orchestration; portable between clouds. Amazon EKS, ECS, Google GKE Image provenance and scanning
DBaaS Managed databases with automated backups and patching. Amazon RDS, Azure SQL Schema, access control, what gets backed up and for how long
SECaaS Cloud-delivered security: email filtering, WAF, identity, SIEM. Okta, Entra ID, Cloudflare, Sentinel Policy, alert triage, who is allowed to use it

The deck's framing: no servers to patch, but permissions and code must be secure. Same sentence for all four.

04

Four deployment models, and the one that is not quite a model

Service model answers how much you hand over. Deployment model answers whose building it sits in.

Public
Owned by a provider, shared by many customers over the internet. Elastic and pay-per-use — AWS, Azure, Google Cloud.
shared
Private
Dedicated to one organisation, on-premises or hosted. More control, higher cost — Eucalyptus, OpenStack, VMware.
dedicated
Community
Shared by organisations with common requirements: government agencies, universities, a sector regulator.
shared purpose
Hybrid
Two or more clouds connected so data and apps move between them — private core banking plus a public web front end.
connected
Multi-cloud
Services from several public providers, to avoid lock-in or take best-of-breed. Not a deployment model so much as a procurement habit.
plural

Choosing a deployment is a trade, not a ranking

No column wins every row. Pick the rows that matter and the model usually chooses itself.

06

A community cloud that exists because of regulation

AWS GovCloud (US), launched 2011: an isolated region for US agencies and their contractors.

US agencies wanted cloud agility but had strict rules about who may touch sensitive data. AWS built isolated regions operated by vetted US personnel and designed around regulations such as ITAR and FedRAMP High. Agencies and contractors with similar compliance needs share that region — a community-cloud model with a government boundary around it. Azure and Google offer equivalents, and sovereign clouds elsewhere follow the same pattern.

The lesson is not about AWS. It is that deployment models get chosen for legal reasons at least as often as for cost. Kenya's Data Protection Act requires some public-interest processing to stay in-country, which is a deployment constraint wearing a compliance costume.

the question I would put to a vendor

“Which region, whose personnel, which certification, and who audits it?” A community cloud is only as strong as the membership rules — if any member can escalate, the boundary is decorative.

07

Choosing for a real scenario

Mwangaza University: 12,000 students, two system administrators, a small budget, and a registrar with a legal requirement.

Three workloads, three answers
Workload Model Why What the university still owns
Staff and student email SaaS A commodity application; running it yourself would consume both administrators. User lifecycle, MFA, sharing settings, retention
Custom PHP records system Private cloud or IaaS, in-country Registrar requires records to stay under university control and comply with the Data Protection Act. Everything from the OS up; the data always
Research simulations, a few times a year Public IaaS, on demand Burst capacity paid for only during simulation season; buying a cluster would idle for months. The images, the jobs, the credentials, the spend cap

And the fourth workload is not a workload: email in SaaS plus records in private plus research in public is, together, a hybrid deployment — which means somebody now has to own the links between the three, not just the three.

08

What you still have to secure, by model

The stack tells you what is yours. This is what to actually do with it.

IaaS and PaaS

  • IaaS: patch and harden the operating system.
  • IaaS: configure firewalls — security groups — and encrypt disks.
  • IaaS: protect SSH keys and instance credentials.
  • PaaS: secure application code, dependencies and secrets.
  • PaaS: configure platform settings — TLS, logging, scaling limits.
  • Both: least-privilege IAM roles and network segmentation.

SaaS

  • Manage the user lifecycle: joiners, movers, leavers.
  • Enforce MFA and single sign-on.
  • Review sharing settings — public links, external sharing.
  • Check the provider's certifications: ISO 27001, SOC 2.
  • Back up critical SaaS data — providers may not restore deleted items forever.

security impact

Notice which list is longer. The SaaS column has no patching in it at all, yet it is where most organisations lose data — through a shared link, a departed employee's live session, or a mailbox nobody backed up.

Lab: one app, three models — and the matrix as data

The lab needs three accounts I do not have. What I could do was make the week's most important diagram machine-checkable.

labs/week03 · serve_compare.py, nginx.conf, site/hello.html, stack_model.py · logs: serve_compare.log, stack_check.log

The account half

not run  Launch an EC2 instance and install nginx, deploy the same page on Elastic Beanstalk, build an equivalent form in Google Forms, then compare setup time and what each model did for you. That needs an AWS account plus a Google or Microsoft account, so it is listed as the next practical step rather than written up as if I had done it.

The half I could actually run

Without an AWS or a Google account I cannot launch EC2 or build the Google Form. But the comparison the lab is really after — what each model does for you, and what it leaves you holding — I could measure locally, with one page served two ways: a server I configured, and one that configures itself.

The timings are noise: both are serving within a tenth of a second and answering in about a millisecond. The rows that matter are the other three. The one-line server announces its exact runtime to whoever asks and hands out a directory listing to whoever guesses a folder name — and there is no file to edit, because there is no configuration; changing that means giving up the one-liner. The 21 lines I wrote buy server_tokens off, autoindex off and a document root I chose. That trade is the module in miniature: in IaaS the defaults are mine to get wrong; in PaaS they are someone else's to have thought about.

what the harness taught me

The first run left nginx answering on port 8081 long after the script had finished. nginx daemonises, so the process I launched exited immediately and my cleanup was watching a dead parent. The fix was one line — daemon off; — so the process I start is the process I can stop. Half of operations is knowing which process you are actually talking to.

The script exits non-zero if the page and the model ever disagree, so a stray edit to a table cell fails loudly instead of quietly becoming a wrong diagram. That is the same instinct as a test: the diagram is a claim, and claims should be checked.

security impact

This is also how cloud configuration should be handled. One definition — the policy, the Terraform, the matrix — and everything else derived from it, checked in CI. Hand-maintained copies drift, and in security a drifted copy is a hole with a plausible justification.

Five questions on Mwangaza University

Email, a records system the registrar wants to keep, and a research cluster used a few times a year.

Q1Which model best fits staff and student email?

B. SaaS. Email is a commodity application. Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace minimises administrative work — and leaves user lifecycle, MFA and sharing settings with the university.

Q2Engineering wants large computers only during simulation season. The best fit is:

C. Public IaaS, on demand. On-demand instances are burst capacity paid only while used. Buying a cluster means paying for idle hardware eleven months a year.

Q3If the student records system moves to PaaS, who patches the operating system?

A. The provider. In PaaS the provider manages the OS and runtime. The university secures its code, its configuration and its data — not the machine underneath.

Q4A shared platform for several universities' library services is an example of:

B. Community cloud. Organisations with a common purpose and common requirements share the infrastructure. The strength of it depends entirely on the membership rules.

Q5Keeping records on a private cloud while email and research use public cloud is:

A. A hybrid deployment. Private and public clouds working together is hybrid. The costs arrive in the joins: identity across both, encrypted links, and knowing which data may cross.

What I am taking from module 03

  1. 01IaaS gives the most control and the most security work; SaaS the least of both.
  2. 02Data, identities and access are always the customer's responsibility — the top row never moves.
  3. 03Serverless and containers shift more layers to the provider, but not your code or your permissions.
  4. 04Deployment models trade control, cost and compliance, and are often chosen for legal reasons.
  5. 05Hybrid and multi-cloud add flexibility and integration complexity: the links need an owner.
