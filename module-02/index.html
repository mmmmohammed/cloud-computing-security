Module 02 — cloud fundamentals

“Cloud” is a definition, not a metaphor.

NIST SP 800-145 defines cloud with five essential characteristics, three service models and four deployment models. Those numbers decide what is shared, who controls what, and where new risk appears. This module takes the definition apart, then looks at the machine actually running underneath it.

01

The definition is a test you can fail

“Minimal management effort”, “shared pool”, “rapidly provisioned and released” — each phrase rules something out.

NIST's wording is deliberately operational. A machine you rent by the month, provision by ticket, and cannot release when you are done satisfies none of it. Read the phrases as requirements:

What each phrase in the NIST definition excludes
Phrase What it requires What it excludes
On-demand network access You reach it over standard network protocols, when you ask A service only reachable from the provider's site
Shared pool of resources Capacity is pooled and allocated to many customers Hardware dedicated to one tenant by design
Rapidly provisioned and released Capacity appears and disappears in minutes, at scale A procurement cycle measured in weeks
Minimal management effort The customer does not build or maintain the physical layer A contract where you still rack the servers

why the definition matters for security

Each phrase names something the customer no longer controls. No physical access, no rack-level isolation, no view of the network between tenants — so assurance has to come from contracts, configuration and logging instead. That is the whole shape of cloud security, and it is decided here, in week two.

02

Five characteristics, and the risk each one quietly creates

Every convenience in the left of this table has a matching failure mode. They are not opposites — they are the same feature seen from two sides.

The five essential characteristics (NIST SP 800-145)
Characteristic What it gives you What it costs you in security
On-demand self-service Users provision servers or storage themselves through a portal or API — no waiting for IT. Shadow IT: resources that exist, cost money and appear in no inventory.
Broad network access Services are reached over the network from phones, laptops and servers using standard protocols. Every endpoint is exposed to the internet; authentication becomes the front door.
Resource pooling Many tenants share hardware; physical location is abstracted away. Multi-tenancy risk: isolation rests on the hypervisor, and you cannot see your neighbours.
Rapid elasticity Capacity grows and shrinks quickly, often automatically, to match demand. Bill shock and untracked assets: the estate changes faster than the register does.
Measured service Usage is metered — CPU hours, GB stored, requests — for billing and reporting. Not a risk but a gift: the same metering is evidence for detection and anomaly alerts.

Self-test

Names first, definitions hidden. Open each one and check yourself rather than reading down the list.

01On-demand self-service

Users provision servers or storage themselves through a portal or API, with no waiting for IT and no human in the loop.

resources nobody tracks. Self-service without a permission boundary is how organisations discover they own twelve test databases.

02Broad network access

Services are reachable over the network from phones, laptops and servers, using standard protocols.

the attack surface is the internet. Anything reachable is scanned continuously, usually within minutes.

03Resource pooling

The provider serves many customers from shared hardware, and abstracts where that hardware physically sits.

isolation depends entirely on the hypervisor. A hypervisor flaw can break it between tenants — a VM escape.

04Rapid elasticity

Capacity grows and shrinks quickly, often automatically, to match demand.

cost and inventory both move faster than people do. The bill and the asset register drift apart.

05Measured service

Usage is metered — CPU hours, gigabytes stored, requests served — for billing, reporting and control.

the opposite of one: metering is the cheapest detection signal in the cloud. A sudden egress spike usually shows up on a bill before it shows up in an alert.

03

Virtualisation is the engine, and the hypervisor is the lock

Resource pooling is not a business idea. It is a software layer that pretends one machine is many.

Applications
Web servers, databases and business apps running inside each virtual machine.
tenant
Guest operating systems
Each VM has its own OS — Linux, Windows — isolated from its neighbours.
tenant
Hypervisor
Creates and isolates the VMs, and shares CPU, memory and I/O between them.
provider
Physical hardware
Servers, storage and network in the provider's data centre.
provider
Two hypervisor types
Type Runs on Examples Where you meet it
Type 1 — bare metal The hardware directly KVM, Xen, VMware ESXi, Hyper-V Public clouds; AWS Nitro is built on KVM technology
Type 2 — hosted A host operating system VirtualBox, VMware Workstation Laptops, labs and — as it happens — the machine writing this

Containers are the other half of the same idea: they share the host kernel instead of carrying a full guest OS. Lighter, faster to start, and weaker isolation — there is no hypervisor boundary between two containers on the same host.

security impact

Resource pooling only works because the hypervisor enforces separation. A hypervisor flaw is therefore not one customer's problem — it is everyone on that host. This is why provider patching and host-level isolation are things you ask a vendor about, in writing.

04

What happens when you click “Launch instance”

Five steps, none of them involving a person at the provider. Open each one for what to watch at that stage.

01Request

You submit a request in the console, CLI or API, with your credentials attached.

the credentials themselves. A key committed to a public repository is a launch request waiting for someone else to send.

02Authenticate and authorise

IAM establishes who you are and whether policy allows the action — before anything is created.

over-broad policy. “Allow * on *” means one stolen key is the whole account, so this is the step where least privilege is either real or theatre.

03Schedule

The control plane picks a host with free capacity, in the zone you asked for.

nothing you can see. Placement is the provider's decision, which is exactly why “where is my data?” has to be answered by region, not by machine.

04Provision

The hypervisor creates the VM and attaches storage and network. This is the part that used to take weeks.

the defaults. A network interface with a public address and an open security group is a provisioning choice, made in seconds, that decides your exposure.

05Meter and log

Usage starts billing, and the API call is written to the audit log.

the log is only evidence if it is on and retained. CloudTrail-style history is the difference between an incident you can reconstruct and one you can only describe.

the sentence this section exists for

Every step is software. That is why cloud security is largely about identity, configuration and logging — the three layers an attacker moves through after the network stops being the boundary.

05

Traditional IT and cloud, on the factors that actually differ

Cloud is not automatically cheaper or safer. It changes how cost and security are managed.

On-premises compared with cloud
Factor On-premises Cloud
Cost model Capital expenditure: buy servers up front Operating expenditure: pay as you go
Provisioning time Weeks to months — procure, ship, install Minutes, through a portal or API
Scaling Limited by hardware already bought Elastic; scale out or in on demand
Maintenance Your team patches hardware and facilities The provider maintains the physical layer
Security responsibility Entirely yours Shared between provider and customer
Geographic reach Your own sites only Many regions worldwide, in minutes
Control and visibility Full physical control Less physical control; rely on provider assurance

06

Benefits, and the risks that arrive with them

Most of the right-hand column is the customer's responsibility. That is the point of the module, and of week seven.

What adoption buys

  • Agility: launch new services in days, not quarters.
  • Elastic scale for peaks — exam results, Black Friday.
  • Lower upfront cost for start-ups and SMEs.
  • Built-in redundancy across zones and regions.
  • Access to advanced services: AI, analytics, managed security.
  • Global reach for diaspora and export customers.

What it hands you

  • Misconfiguration exposing data publicly.
  • Account takeover through weak credentials.
  • Reduced visibility and control over the physical layer.
  • Data sovereignty and legal compliance issues.
  • Vendor lock-in and unexpected costs.
  • Dependence on internet connectivity and provider uptime.

07

Elasticity under pressure: Pokémon GO, July 2016

Niantic ran the backend on Google Cloud — Kubernetes Engine and Cloud Datastore. Then the game shipped.

original target 1×
worst-case estimate 10×
launch peak 50×

Traffic figures from the Google Cloud blog case study, as cited in the module material.

Engineers scaled the container clusters and load balancing quickly to keep the game playable, and players still saw outages in some regions while capacity caught up. A comparable on-premises launch would have needed months to buy hardware.

security impact

Elasticity only helps if the whole path can scale — quotas, databases, load balancers and the bill. Sudden scale also widens the attack surface: more instances, more endpoints, more things created by automation and never reviewed. A university portal on admission-results day has exactly the same shape.

security impact

The same 42 regions are also 42 legal jurisdictions and 42 different data residency answers. Choosing a region is a compliance decision made by whoever types the region code, which is usually a developer, at 11pm, without asking.

10

Five questions on the Duka Online case

A Nairobi electronics shop, two rented servers, and one Black Friday that ended in an hour.

Q1Servers that start automatically when traffic rises demonstrate which NIST characteristic?

D. Rapid elasticity. Capacity scaling out and in quickly, often automatically, is the definition of elasticity.

Q2A dashboard showing the cost of each service relies mainly on:

A. Measured service. Metering is what produces per-service cost reporting. Without it there is nothing to put on the dashboard.

Q3The finance director's worry that “we don't know which machine our data is on” relates to:

D. Resource pooling with location independence. Pooling abstracts the physical machine; customers normally choose only a region, which is why “where is it?” has to be answered in policy rather than by pointing at a server.

Q4The best control for the “anyone can create servers” risk is:

B. Budgets and alerts plus IAM permissions. Self-service should be governed, not removed: permissions decide who may act, budgets bound what it can cost.

Q5Moving from buying servers to paying monthly for usage changes the cost model from:

D. CapEx to OpEx. Capital expenditure on hardware becomes operating expenditure on services — which is why cost control becomes a continuous job rather than a purchase decision.

What I am taking from module 02

  1. 01NIST defines cloud with five characteristics, three service models and four deployment models — and the definition is testable, not decorative.
  2. 02Virtualisation and hypervisors let many tenants share hardware if isolation holds; the hypervisor is the lock, and its flaws are everybody's.
  3. 03Cloud moves cost from CapEx to OpEx and provisioning from weeks to minutes.
  4. 04Every cloud action is an API call: identity, configuration and logging are the security surface.
  5. 05The benefits are real and so are the risks — and most of the risks land on the customer, not the provider.
